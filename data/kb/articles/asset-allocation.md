# Asset Allocation and Rebalancing

Asset allocation is how a portfolio is divided among asset classes such as stocks, bonds and
cash. It is one of the most important decisions an investor makes, because it drives most of
the portfolio's ups and downs.

## The main asset classes

- **Stocks:** highest long-term growth potential, largest swings.
- **Bonds:** steadier income, smaller swings, lower expected growth; often hold up better when
  stocks fall.
- **Cash and cash equivalents:** savings and money market funds; stable, but at risk of losing
  ground to inflation.

## Choosing a mix

The right mix depends on your time horizon and risk tolerance. Examples are often described as
splits such as 80/20 (80% stocks, 20% bonds) for long horizons, 60/40 for a balanced approach,
or 30/70 for someone who needs stability. A **three-fund portfolio**, a US stock fund, an
international stock fund and a bond fund, is a simple way to cover the main asset classes.

## Allocation vs diversification

Asset allocation spreads money **across** asset classes; diversification spreads it **within**
each one, for example across hundreds of companies and many sectors. A portfolio needs both: an
80/20 mix built from three individual stocks is still concentrated.

## Rebalancing

Over time, different returns push a portfolio away from its target. After a strong year for
stocks, a 60/40 portfolio might drift to 70/30, carrying more risk than intended.
**Rebalancing** means selling some of what grew and adding to what lagged to return to the
target. People commonly rebalance on a schedule, such as once a year, or when an asset class
drifts more than about five percentage points.

## Glide paths

Some plans shift gradually towards bonds as a goal approaches. Target-date funds do this
automatically, becoming more conservative each year until the target date.
