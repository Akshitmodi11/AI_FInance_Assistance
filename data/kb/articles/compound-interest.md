# How Compound Interest Works

Compound interest is interest earned on interest. Each period, the growth you already earned is
added to your balance, so the next period's growth is calculated on a bigger number. Over long
periods this snowball effect, not the size of the first deposit, does most of the work.

## Simple vs compound interest

With **simple interest**, you earn a return only on the original amount. $1,000 at 5% simple
interest earns $50 every year, so after 10 years you have $1,500.

With **compound interest**, the $50 from year one also earns 5% in year two, and so on. The same
$1,000 at 5% compounded yearly grows to about $1,629 after 10 years and about $4,322 after 30
years. The extra money is entirely growth on earlier growth.

## Why time matters more than you think

Because growth builds on itself, the last years of a long period add far more money than the
first years. Someone who starts saving ten years earlier can end up with much more, even if
they put in less money in total. Starting early and staying invested are usually more powerful
than finding a slightly higher return.

## The rule of 72

A quick way to estimate how long money takes to double: divide 72 by the annual return in
percent. At 6% a year, money doubles in about 72 / 6 = 12 years; at 8%, about 9 years. It is an
approximation, but it makes the effect of different returns easy to compare.

## Compounding works against you on debt

The same maths applies to what you owe. Credit-card balances compound at high interest rates,
so an unpaid balance can grow quickly. Fees also compound: a 1% yearly fund fee takes 1% of a
balance that would otherwise have kept growing, which adds up to a large amount over decades.
