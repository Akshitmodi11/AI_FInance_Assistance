# Inflation and Purchasing Power

Inflation is the general rise in prices over time. When prices rise, each dollar buys less, so
the **purchasing power** of money falls. Inflation is why money that sits still slowly loses
value, even though the number in the account does not change.

## Nominal vs real returns

A **nominal** return is the number you see: a savings account paying 4%. The **real** return
subtracts inflation: if prices rose 3% that year, your real return was only about 1%. If an
account pays less than inflation, your real return is negative and you are losing buying power.
Long-term plans should be judged in real terms.

## Inflation risk

Inflation risk is the chance that your investments do not grow fast enough to keep up with
rising prices. It is easy to overlook because it never shows up as a loss on a statement. Cash
and many low-yield savings products carry little risk of losing dollars but a real risk of
losing purchasing power over decades.

## How inflation changes a goal

A goal that costs $40,000 today will cost more in the future. At 3% yearly inflation, prices
roughly double in about 24 years. Planning tools often show a goal in "today's dollars" or
adjust the target for inflation so the savings plan stays realistic.

## What people do about it

Over long periods, stocks have historically grown faster than inflation, though with large ups
and downs along the way. Some bonds are designed for this risk, such as US Treasury
Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS), whose principal adjusts with inflation. Keeping short-term
money safe and long-term money invested is a common way to balance stability against inflation.
