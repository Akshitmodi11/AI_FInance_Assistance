# Bull Markets, Bear Markets and Corrections

Markets move in long waves of rising and falling prices. Investors use a few common labels to
describe these phases. Knowing them helps put headlines in context.

## Bull market

A bull market is a long period when prices rise and investors are generally optimistic. There
is no single official definition, but it is often described as a rise of 20% or more from a
recent low. Bull markets have historically lasted longer than bear markets.

## Bear market

A bear market is a decline of **20% or more** from a recent high, usually lasting months and
often linked to economic slowdowns or recessions. Pessimism is high, and it can be tempting to
sell. Historically, US stock markets have eventually recovered from every bear market, though
recoveries have sometimes taken years.

## Correction

A correction is a smaller drop of about **10% to 20%** from a recent high. Corrections are
fairly common and often short. Not every correction turns into a bear market.

## Why the labels matter less than they seem

Nobody can reliably predict when a bull market will end or a bear market will bottom. Investors
who sell during a decline and wait for things to "feel safe" often miss the early part of the
recovery, which can include some of the strongest days. For long-term goals, a plan that
expects downturns, with diversification and an emergency fund, tends to work better than trying
to time the market.

## Where trend indicators fit

Tools like moving averages describe what prices have done, for example whether a price is above
its 50-day average. They describe the past trend; they do not forecast the next move.
