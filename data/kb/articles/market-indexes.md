# Market Indexes Explained

A market index tracks the performance of a group of investments, giving one number that
summarises how part of the market is doing. When the news says "the market was up 1% today,"
it usually means a major index rose 1%.

## The best-known US indexes

- **S&P 500:** about 500 large US companies; widely used as the benchmark for the US stock
  market.
- **Dow Jones Industrial Average:** 30 large, well-known US companies; one of the oldest
  indexes.
- **Nasdaq Composite:** thousands of stocks listed on the Nasdaq exchange, with a heavy weight
  in technology companies.

Other indexes track small companies, international markets, bonds or single sectors.

## How indexes are weighted

Most indexes, including the S&P 500, are **market-cap weighted**: bigger companies count more,
so a few very large companies can drive much of the index's move. The Dow is **price
weighted**, so a stock with a higher share price has more influence regardless of company size.

## You cannot buy an index directly

An index is a measurement, not an investment. To get its performance, investors use an
**index fund** or **ETF** that holds the same stocks. Funds that track an index usually have
low fees because they do not pay managers to pick stocks.

## Indexes as benchmarks

Indexes are used to judge performance. If a fund of large US stocks returned 7% while the S&P
500 returned 10%, it lagged its benchmark. Many actively managed funds trail their benchmark
over long periods after fees, which is one reason index funds are popular.

## ETF tickers you may see

Finnie's market overview uses ETFs as stand-ins for indexes: SPY tracks the S&P 500, QQQ tracks
the Nasdaq-100 (the 100 largest non-financial Nasdaq companies), and DIA tracks the Dow.
