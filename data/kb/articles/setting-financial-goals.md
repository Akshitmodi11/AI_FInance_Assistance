# Setting and Planning Financial Goals

A financial goal turns a wish ("I want a house someday") into a plan ("$40,000 for a down
payment in six years"). Once a goal has an amount and a date, you can work out how much to save
each month and how much risk makes sense.

## Make goals specific

A useful goal has a target amount, a target date, and a reason it matters to you. Writing them
down and ranking them helps when money is limited: an emergency fund and high-interest debt
usually come before longer-term wants.

## Short, medium and long term

- **Short term (under about 3 years):** a holiday, a car, an emergency fund. Keep this money
  in savings accounts or other low-risk places.
- **Medium term (about 3 to 10 years):** a home down payment or further education. Often a mix
  of bonds and stocks.
- **Long term (10+ years):** retirement or a child's university. More time allows more stock
  exposure.

## Working out the monthly saving

The amount you need to save each month depends on four inputs: the target, the time you have,
what you already have saved, and the return you assume. More time or a higher assumed return
lowers the monthly amount; a bigger target raises it. Planning tools compute this with the
future-value formula rather than guessing.

## Plan with a range, not one number

Returns are never guaranteed, so a good plan looks at several scenarios: a low, an expected and
a high return. If the plan only works in the high scenario, it may need a longer timeline,
larger contributions or a smaller target. Finnie's Goals tab shows these three scenarios side by
side.

## Revisit regularly

Check progress once or twice a year and after big life changes. Adjust contributions if you are
behind, and consider reducing risk as the target date gets close.
